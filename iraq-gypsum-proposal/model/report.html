How to read this document

Every figure in this memorandum derives from a single engineering and financial model built from first principles — mass balance, energy balance, and a line-item capital cost build-up — rather than from top-down rules of thumb. Where a figure is sourced, the source is named in Appendix A. Where it is an engineering estimate, it is described as such.

The model is built in constant 2026 US dollars. No general inflation is applied, on the assumption that nominal selling prices and nominal costs escalate together in the long run. Only real divergences are modelled: a one-off competitive step-down in realised price over the first four operating years, and real wage growth of 1.5% per year. The discount rate is therefore a real rate.

1Executive summary

Iraq consumes roughly 25 million square metres of plasterboard a year and manufactures almost none of it. Around 70% arrives overland from Turkey, carrying 1,300 kilometres of road freight, 15% of duty and levy, and an importer's margin before it reaches a Baghdad builders' merchant. Iraq simultaneously sits on some of the largest and most accessible gypsum deposits in the Middle East. That gap between a resource-rich country and an import-dependent market is the investment case.

What is proposed

A vertically integrated plant in the Anbar–Baghdad corridor combining a gypsum quarry concession, a 110 thousand tonne per year calcination plant, and a 12.0 million m² per year wallboard line, extended in Year 4 with steel framing profiles and jointing compounds to sell a complete wall system rather than a commodity sheet. Sustainable output is 10.0 million m² per year at a realistic 70% overall equipment effectiveness.

The economics

The plant produces board at a total cash cost of $1.313 per m² against a landed Turkish import wholesale price of roughly $2.78 per m² in Baghdad. That is a wide cost umbrella. At a target ex-works price of $2.21 — some 20% below landed import — the business earns a 59% contribution margin and, at steady state, $8.1M of EBITDA on $33.5M of revenue. On board and plaster alone, before the lower-margin profiles business dilutes the ratio, the EBITDA margin is 38%.

Three findings the investor must weigh before anything else

1. The brief does not fit the budget — but the equity cheque does. A fully integrated 12 million m² plant with a complete wall-system offer cannot be built for under $20 million. It costs $36.1M all-in. Capped at $20 million of total project cost, an integrated plant tops out at 4.6 million m² — 38% of the target — at a 58% worse capital intensity. The resolution is not to shrink the plant but to finance it: with $8.8M of ECA-backed buyer credit against the Chinese equipment package and $7.8M of local and vendor debt, the sponsor equity requirement is $19.6M — inside the envelope.

2. A competitor is already pursuing this exact project. Al Tiraz Al Sharqi Global has published a $63.8 million plan for an integrated mine at Hit and a factory outside Ramadi producing 40 million m² of wallboard, 600,000 tonnes of powder, ceiling tile and metal parts. Same governorate, same corridor, same strategy, four times the capacity. If it is built as described, domestic capacity would reach 50 million m² against roughly 36 million m² of 2032 demand.

3. The returns are real but not comfortable. A 15.5% real ungeared IRR against a 13.5% real WACC is a positive but thin spread for Iraqi country risk. A 10% fall in realised price takes the IRR to 8.8% and the NPV negative. This is a project that must be executed quickly and well, or not at all.

Recommendation in brief

Proceed to a defined and time-boxed feasibility stage, not to construction. Commit approximately $850,000 and six months to resource drilling, vendor bid analysis, a firm ECA financing term sheet, and — most importantly — direct verification of the competitor's status and of actual Iraqi market prices and volumes, which cannot be established from public sources. Section 16 sets out the specific gates.

The brief specified a 10 million m² integrated plant with a full wall system for under $20 million. Those three requirements are mutually inconsistent. This section shows by how much, and how to resolve it.

Why small plants are disproportionately expensive

Plasterboard manufacturing has severe scale economics. A dryer, a forming station, a control system, a laboratory, a weighbridge and a plant manager cost almost the same whether the line runs at 5 million or 30 million m² per year. Halving capacity does not halve capital cost — it reduces it by roughly a third, while fixed operating cost per m² rises in direct proportion. This is why the $20M-capped integrated variant carries a capital intensity of $4.05 per annual m² against $2.57 at full scale: the constrained plant is 58% less capital-efficient per unit of output.

Cross-checking the capital estimate

An independent check is available. Al Tiraz Al Sharqi Global has disclosed a total investment of $63.8 million, of which $10 million is working capital, for an integrated complex producing 40 million m² of wallboard plus 600,000 tonnes of powder, ceiling tile and metal parts. Scaling that $53.8 million of fixed capital down to this project's output on a 0.65 power law implies roughly $24.5 million of fixed capital, against this study's bottom-up estimate of $30.8M for Phase 1.

The 25% gap is explicable and, in this analyst's view, sits in the right direction. This study carries a full 10% contingency, complete site infrastructure for a greenfield location with no existing services, 5 MW of captive generation, and interest during construction. A promoter's investment figure prepared to attract capital would not ordinarily carry all of those. The estimate here should be treated as realistic and the competitor's as optimistic, but neither is a substitute for firm vendor quotations.

The resolution

Do not shrink the plant to fit the equity. Finance it. The Chinese-origin equipment package is $10.3M, which qualifies for Sinosure-backed buyer credit at approximately 85% — $8.8M at around 6.5% over ten years with a three-year grace period. Adding $7.8M of local bank and vendor financing brings total debt to $16.5M, or 46% gearing, and reduces the sponsor's equity cheque to $19.6M.

Debt service is comfortable once the plant is running — average DSCR over the life of the debt is 3.4x — but the first production year cannot service its own debt (DSCR 0.61x). A six-month debt service reserve account funded at financial close is a non-negotiable condition.

3Market analysis

Macro and construction context

Iraq has a population of approximately 46 million growing at about 2.5% per year, one of the youngest age structures in the region, and a construction sector that grew 5.5% in real terms in 2025 and is forecast to grow 4.8% per year through 2029, supported by investment in energy, water infrastructure and the government's target of lifting oil production above 6 million barrels per day. Government has sought to attract roughly $7 billion of investment to stimulate growth. Cement output exceeded 10 million tonnes in 2024, confirming both the scale of construction activity and the viability of heavy building-materials manufacturing in-country.

The housing deficit is the demand engine

Iraq's housing gap exceeded 3 million units by the end of 2024, with credible estimates ranging from 2.5 to 3.5 million. Roughly 250,000 new units per year are needed simply to hold the deficit constant against population growth. Sixteen new residential cities are under construction or in preparation, and the 2025–2030 national plan targets halving the housing crisis. Execution has been weak — around 65% of government housing projects were delayed or abandoned as of 2023 — which argues for discounting official targets heavily, as this study does.

Iraqi plasterboard demand, 2026 — bottom-up build
Segment build rather than top-down extrapolation. Total 24.6 million m², implying 0.54 m² per capita — consistent with a market at an early stage of drywall adoption (United States roughly 11 m² per capita, developing Asia 1–2 m², regional peers 0.5–0.8 m²).

Reading the demand estimate honestly

This is the weakest number in the study and it deserves to be flagged as such. No reliable published statistic exists for Iraqi plasterboard consumption. The 24.6 million m² figure is built segment by segment from housing completions, dwelling-stock renovation cycles, commercial fit-out, institutional building, post-conflict reconstruction and informal self-build, each with an assumed drywall penetration rate. A reasonable range around it is 18 to 30 million m².

The distinction that matters commercially is that Iraq is a ceiling market before it is a partition market. Decorative suspended and false ceilings are near-universal in Iraqi residential construction, while interior partitions remain predominantly block and hand-applied plaster. That shapes the product mix assumed in Section 7 — a deliberately high 20% share of 9.5 mm ceiling board — and it means the growth opportunity lies in converting partition construction from block to drywall, which is a specification and training problem as much as a price problem.

Market growth against plant output, 2026–2033
Demand grown at 6.5% per year, marginally above the construction sector's forecast 4.8% to reflect rising drywall penetration from a low base. Plant sustainable output shown flat at 9.98 million m².

At sustainable output the plant would hold roughly 40% of the 2026 market, falling to about 28% of the 2032 market as demand grows. A 28% national share for the only integrated domestic producer is ambitious but not unreasonable; a 40% share on day one is not achievable, which is why the model ramps utilisation over five years and reaches only 83% of nameplate.

Al Tiraz Al Sharqi Global — the decisive competitive fact

ATS is a Baghdad building-materials trading company, founded 2005 and family-owned, which has published detailed plans for an integrated gypsum mine and factory in Anbar: a 2.5 million m² mine concession at Hit, a 500,000 m² factory site outside Ramadi 135 km from Baghdad, and a stated investment of $63.8 million including $10 million of working capital. At capacity it intends to produce 600,000 tonnes of gypsum powder, 40 million m² of wallboard, 5 million m² of ceiling tile and 21.6 million metal parts.

This is the same governorate, the same highway corridor, the same vertically integrated strategy and the same product range contemplated here, at roughly four times the scale. It is also, notably, the same commercial logic — an incumbent building-materials distributor integrating backwards into manufacturing — which is the natural winning structure in this market and one this project does not have.

Implication. If ATS builds as described, combined domestic capacity reaches 50 million m² against roughly 36 million m² of 2032 demand — a 72% industry utilisation that would collapse pricing. The sensitivity analysis in Section 12 shows a 20% price fall drives this project's IRR to negative. Verifying ATS's actual construction status, financing and timetable is the single highest-value piece of confirmatory diligence in this entire study.

Strategic options with respect to ATS

  1. Race and win on speed. Commission inside 24 months, lock distribution and specification before ATS reaches market. Viable only if ATS is still pre-financing.
  2. Partner. Take an equity position in, or form a joint venture with, ATS. One 40 million m² integrated plant serving Iraq is a far better asset than two plants fighting for a 36 million m² market. This deserves serious consideration.
  3. Differentiate. Concede volume board and concentrate on the higher-margin system — moisture and fire-rated board, profiles, compounds and technical service — where distribution and specification matter more than tonnage.
  4. Decline. If ATS is financed and under construction, the rational decision is not to build a second plant into the same market.

6Location and site selection

The gypsum resource

Iraq's primary gypsum reserve is estimated at 142 million tonnes, with gypsum and anhydrite of the Middle Miocene Fatha Formation outcropping at surface across Nineveh, Kirkuk, Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, Diyala, Wasit and Anbar. Roughly 130 million tonnes are attributed to Anbar and Basrah. The Iraq Geological Survey has begun new exploration programmes for gypsum in Anbar and Nineveh. The Hit–Kubaisa belt in Anbar, some 70 km from Ramadi, is the historic centre of Iraqi gypsum extraction and supplies the country's plaster industry today.

Two geological caveats matter commercially. First, Fatha Formation gypsum carries accessory anhydrite, dolomite, quartz and calcite, and purity varies within and between beds — the model assumes 90% ore purity and selective mining. Second, gypsum is a soft mineral (Mohs 2) and is generally rippable, which removes drilling and blasting from the mining cost and is the reason contract extraction at around $4.20 per tonne is achievable.

The instinctive answer is to build at the quarry. The analysis does not support it.

Why the quarry site loses

Because haulage turns out not to matter very much. Total road logistics — rock inbound plus finished goods outbound — varies by only $0.27 million per year across the three leading sites, on an annual revenue base above $33 million. Siting at the quarry saves the rock haul but adds an equal and opposite finished-goods haul to Baghdad, while sacrificing grid access, labour availability, security and customer proximity.

Recommendation — Ramadi outskirts, Highway 11 corridor

Ramadi balances the two hauls at close to the theoretical optimum (within $0.10 million per year of the best case), sits 70 km from the Hit–Kubaisa gypsum belt and 110 km from Baghdad on the trunk road to Jordan, and offers materially better power, labour and security than a remote quarry site. Its logistics cost of $3.50 per tonne of rock is carried explicitly in the model, raising stucco cost from $15.76 to $20.62 per tonne.

Alternate: Amiriyat al-Fallujah, which scores highest on the qualitative matrix (7.56 against 7.27) and sits closest to the Baghdad demand centre, at $0.17 million per year of additional logistics cost. If a gypsum concession can be secured within 90 km, Amiriyat becomes the preferred site.

It is worth noting that ATS independently selected the Hit-mine/Ramadi-factory configuration. Convergent site logic from an experienced local operator is corroborating evidence.

Land requirement

Approximately 300,000 m² for the factory (of which 33,000 m² built in Phase 1 and 2) with room for a doubling of capacity, plus a quarry concession of 2 to 3 km². At 166,000 tonnes per year of run-of-mine extraction over a 25-year life, total extraction is 4.2 million tonnes — a modest footprint against Anbar's resource base. Land is held under a 50-year renewable lease under the National Investment Law.

The 70% sustainable OEE is deliberately conservative. World-class plasterboard plants achieve 85–92%. A first-of-kind plant in Iraq with a partly Chinese line, an inexperienced workforce and unreliable grid power will not. Modelling nameplate output as achievable would overstate revenue by 20%.

Process description

  1. Extraction. Open-pit selective mining of Fatha Formation gypsum at Hit–Kubaisa. Soft rock, ripped rather than blasted. Contract-mined at ~$4.20/t.
  2. Crushing and screening. Primary jaw crusher (250 t/h) and secondary impactor at the quarry, reducing to −25 mm. Crushing at the pit minimises haulage volume and protects the calciner from oversize.
  3. Haulage. 70 km to the plant, 166 thousand tonnes per year.
  4. Grinding. Impact/hammer mill with dynamic classifier to land plaster fineness. ~22 kWh/t.
  5. Calcination. Continuous kettle calciner converting gypsum dihydrate to hemihydrate at 150–165 °C. Kettle technology is chosen over flash calcination for its tolerance of feed variability — the correct trade-off for Fatha Formation ore.
  6. Conditioning. Stucco cooled and held in conditioning silos to stabilise rehydration behaviour before use.
  7. Mixing and forming. Stucco, water, foam, starch, retarder, accelerator and dispersant combined in a high-shear pin mixer; slurry deposited between two paper liners at the forming station, which sets board thickness, edge profile and density.
  8. Setting belt. ~120 m belt allowing the core to hydrate and develop green strength before cutting.
  9. Cutting and transfer. Flying knife cuts to length; boards accelerated, turned and booked face-to-face for drying.
  10. Drying. Six-deck ~90 m dryer removing 4.33 kg of free water per m². Two-zone profile: high-temperature evaporation followed by a gentle zone to prevent calcination of the set core.
  11. Finishing. Trim saw, bundling in pairs face-to-face, strapping, labelling, palletising and warehousing.

Utilities

Power. Grid supply in Anbar is not dependable at industrial standard. The design assumes 5 MW of dual-fuel captive generation on an N+1 basis, capable of full island operation, with grid taken opportunistically. The model blends 55% grid at $0.046/kWh with 45% self-generation at $0.090/kWh for an effective $0.0658/kWh.

Thermal. Dryer and calciner burners run on diesel at an assumed $0.28 per litre — above the subsidised pump price of roughly $0.19 to allow for industrial supply and logistics — giving $7.80 per GJ. The single largest identified upside in this study is pipeline natural gas. Anbar hosts the Akkas gas field; gas at $4.50/GJ would cut thermal cost from $0.149 to about $0.086 per m², worth roughly $0.6 million a year. The plant should be designed dual-fuel from the outset.

Water. Boreholes with treatment and 2,000 m³ of storage. Plasterboard manufacture is near zero liquid discharge — process water is consumed into the board or evaporated.

8Equipment supplier evaluation

The brief specified a Chinese main line with European critical components. That is the right answer, and this section sets out precisely where the European money should go — because the choice of which components to upgrade matters more than the decision to upgrade at all.

The strategy

A complete European line from Grenzebach, Siempelkamp or Gyptech would cost more than this project's entire equity budget. A wholly Chinese turnkey line is affordable but concentrates risk in exactly the places where plasterboard plants fail: slurry consistency, board density control, paper-to-core bond, and dryer control. The recommended package spends $1.22 million — about 21% of the board-line budget — on four European sub-systems that govern those four failure modes, while buying the bulk mechanical handling, which is commodity engineering, from a Chinese tier-1 supplier.

Commercial conditions to impose on the main-line supplier

  • Performance guarantee on output rate, board weight tolerance, surface quality and reject rate, tested over a continuous 72-hour run.
  • 10% retention released only on passing the performance test, plus a bank guarantee for the warranty period.
  • Six months of resident commissioning support with named engineers, not a day-rate allowance.
  • Two years of critical spares in the base scope, held on site — Iraqi import lead times make a stocked-out bearing a multi-week outage.
  • Open control architecture — Siemens or ABB PLC with documented code and transferable licences. Proprietary supplier-locked control software is a long-term hostage situation.
  • Training of 12 operators and 4 engineers at a reference plant running the same line, before shipment.

Raw material supply

Facing paper is the critical input — 32% of variable cost, entirely imported, and the material most likely to halt production. Qualify at least two mills (a Turkish supplier for lead time, a European or Asian mill for quality benchmark) before commissioning and hold 90 days of cover in a climate-controlled store. Starch is available from Cargill, Roquette or Tate & Lyle; foaming agents from specialist chemical suppliers; dispersants from BASF or Sika. Ball mill accelerator (BMA) is produced on site from the plant's own gypsum.

What actually drives the cost

Three observations reframe the economics of this business.

Gypsum is nearly free; paper is expensive. Self-produced stucco costs $20.62 per tonne delivered into the mixer, which at 7.06 kg/m² is $0.1454 per m² — just 16% of variable cost. Imported facing paper costs $0.2853 per m², nearly twice as much. The value of backward integration into gypsum is real but modest; the exposure to imported paper is the dominant raw-material risk.

Energy is the second-largest line. Drying and calcination together account for roughly a quarter of variable cost. Subsidised Iraqi fuel is a genuine and durable competitive advantage against Turkish imports, and pipeline gas would extend it.

Fixed cost per m² is the scale argument. At sustainable output, fixed cost is $0.4104 per m². At the 4.6 million m² plant that would fit a $20 million cap, the same fixed base spreads to roughly $0.89 per m² — which alone would consume two-thirds of the cash margin.

Two things the return figures conceal

Leverage barely improves the equity return. Equity IRR (15.1%) is essentially identical to project IRR (15.5%). This is not an error: the debt fully amortises well before the twenty-year terminal date, so over that horizon the gearing effect washes out. The purpose of the debt in this structure is not to enhance returns — it is to reduce the sponsor's cheque from $36.1M to $19.6M. Judge the financing on that basis.

Value is concentrated in the terminal period. Terminal value is $58.27M undiscounted. Over the explicit twenty-year forecast alone, before terminal value, the project's discounted cash flows do not fully recover the investment at a 13.5% discount rate. The project's value therefore rests on the plant still operating profitably beyond year twenty — a reasonable expectation for a plasterboard asset with a captive resource, but a real dependency that a conservative investor should price.

Tax

The model assumes full exemption from corporate income tax for ten years from commercial operation under National Investment Law No. 13 of 2006, extendable to fifteen years for a joint venture with majority Iraqi ownership. Iraq's headline corporate rate of 15% is applied from Year 11. The exemption is materially valuable and should be treated as a condition precedent, not an assumption.

What the sensitivities say

The project is far more sensitive to price than to capital cost. A 25% capital overrun — a large miss — still leaves a 12.6% IRR. A 10% price decline, a much smaller commercial event, takes it to 8.8%. This ordering should govern where management attention goes: securing price and specification matters more than shaving capital.

The base case sits 2.0 percentage points above WACC. That is a positive but narrow margin for a frontier-market industrial project, and it means the project has little tolerance for simultaneous adverse movements. The combined bear case is not an extreme construction: a 15% price fall, a 20% capital overrun and a 15% utilisation shortfall are each individually plausible.

Why the break-even is reassuring

The plant covers its cash costs at 26% of nameplate — below even the first-year ramp target of 35% — and can absorb a 41% price collapse before burning cash. The financing logic

$10.30M of the equipment package is Chinese-origin. Chinese export credit supported by Sinosure will typically finance 85% of the contract value of such a package on ten-year terms with a two-to-three-year grace period, at rates well below Iraqi domestic lending. That single facility contributes $8.76M — the difference between a project the sponsor cannot fund and one it can.

The balance comes from a local bank or vendor facility at a realistic Iraqi rate of 13%. Total gearing of 46% is conservative for an asset-backed industrial project and is supported by an average DSCR of 3.4x.

Conditions the lenders will impose — and should

  • Debt service reserve account funded with six months of principal and interest at financial close. Year-2 DSCR of 0.61x means the first production year cannot service debt from its own cash flow. This is the single most important structuring point in the financing.
  • Completion guarantee from the sponsor until the performance test is passed and sustained output is demonstrated.
  • Equity first. Sponsor equity fully drawn before debt, so the lenders' money goes into a substantially de-risked project.
  • Cash sweep above an agreed DSCR, and dividend lock-up below it.
  • Security over the land lease, plant, mining licence, insurances and project accounts.
  • Political risk and business interruption insurance assigned to lenders.

Phase 2 is self-funded

The steel profiles and jointing compounds lines — $2.81M — are installed in Year 4 from operating cash flow, not from the initial raise. They add roughly $12.5 million of revenue and $2.2 million of EBITDA at a lower margin than board, and earn back their capital in under three years. Deferring them keeps the initial equity requirement inside the envelope while preserving the full wall-system strategy.

None of the following should be treated as a formality. Each is capable of stopping the project, and each is cheaper to resolve now than after financial close.

16Recommendation

Proceed to a time-boxed feasibility stage. Do not commit construction capital until the four gating questions below are answered.

The case for

  • A genuine structural arbitrage: a resource-rich country importing 70% of a product it has the raw materials to make, with roughly $0.93 per m² of freight and duty protection built into the landed import price.
  • A cost position that survives a 41% price collapse before burning cash, and breaks even at 26% of nameplate capacity.
  • Subsidised energy and low labour cost that Turkish and Gulf competitors cannot replicate.
  • A ten-year tax exemption and duty-free capital equipment under the National Investment Law.
  • Demand underwritten by a housing deficit above 2.5 million units and a construction sector growing near 5% a year.
  • The equity requirement, properly financed, fits the sponsor's envelope at $19.6M.

The case against

  • A 15.5% real IRR is a thin spread over a 13.5% real WACC for Iraqi country risk, and the value rests substantially on the terminal period.
  • A named competitor is pursuing an identical strategy at four times the scale in the same governorate. If both are built, the market is oversupplied and pricing collapses.
  • The demand estimate and the price deck — the two assumptions the return is most sensitive to — rest on the weakest evidence in the study.
  • Execution risk is concentrated and unforgiving: a failed ramp, a paper supply interruption or a 10% pricing miss each individually takes the project below its cost of capital.

Four gating questions

1. Is Al Tiraz Al Sharqi financed and building? If yes, restructure as a partnership or stop. This question dominates all others.

2. What do Iraqi buyers actually pay per m² today, and for what volume? If realised prices are more than 10% below this study's deck, the project does not clear its cost of capital.

3. Can $16.5 million of debt be raised on the assumed terms? Without the ECA facility the equity requirement rises above the envelope and the project as scoped is not fundable.

4. Will the sponsor accept a project whose value depends on year-twenty-plus operation? If the investment horizon is under ten years, this is the wrong asset.

This analyst's view

The industrial logic is sound and the cost position is genuinely defensible — this is a real business, not a paper one. But it is a medium-return, high-execution-risk industrial project, not the high-return resource arbitrage it first appears to be, and the returns do not carry enough margin to absorb a determined competitor.

The most attractive version of this opportunity may not be building a second plant at all. It may be taking a position in the one already being developed. That option should be tested before $35 million is committed to competing with it.
